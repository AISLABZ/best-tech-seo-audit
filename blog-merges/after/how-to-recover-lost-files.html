That missing document, photo folder or WhatsApp attachment can feel far more urgent than a cracked screen. Before you download an app, reset a device or keep clicking around, stop. The first rule of how to recover lost files is simple: use the device as little as possible until you know what has happened.

Deleted files are not always gone for good. In many cases, the device has only removed the reference to them and marked the space as available for new data. The more you use it, the greater the chance that space is overwritten. A careful first response can make a real difference.

Start by working out what “lost” means

Files disappear for different reasons, and the right recovery route depends on the cause. You may have accidentally deleted something, emptied the recycle bin, lost access after a software update, or found that your phone, tablet or laptop will no longer switch on. A damaged storage drive or water exposure needs a very different approach from a file that has simply been moved to the wrong folder.

Think back to the last time you saw the file. Did you delete it? Change devices? Sign out of an account? Is the device slow, showing error messages, or asking you to format a drive? These details help identify whether you are dealing with a search problem, a backup problem, a software issue or physical damage.

If the device still works, avoid installing recovery software directly onto the same drive where the files were stored. That installation may overwrite the very data you are trying to retrieve.

Check the places files usually turn up

Before attempting recovery, look in the obvious places methodically. It is surprising how often a file is still there but saved under an unexpected name, moved during a tidy-up, or sitting in a cloud bin.

On a Windows laptop, check the Recycle Bin, Downloads, Documents and Recent files. Use File Explorer search with part of the filename, the file type, or the date it was last edited. On a Mac, check Bin, Recents and search in Finder. Also look for files saved to Desktop, iCloud Drive or an external drive.

On a phone or tablet, open the Photos app and look in Recently Deleted. Check Files, Downloads, Notes and the relevant app itself. Messaging apps may retain attachments within a chat even when the original download has gone. If photos are missing, confirm you are signed into the correct Apple, Google or other cloud account before assuming they have been deleted.

Cloud storage services often keep deleted items for a limited period. This is useful, but do not leave it too long. Retention periods vary, and once a cloud bin is permanently emptied, recovery becomes less certain.

Look for sync and account issues

A file that disappears on one device may still exist on another. If your laptop has stopped syncing, an older phone or tablet could hold a copy. Check whether the file is visible online and whether you have accidentally signed in with a different account.

Be careful when syncing is confused. Do not repeatedly delete and restore folders in an attempt to force it to work. If a sync service is showing conflicts, make a copy of the files that are still accessible before changing settings.

What to do after accidental deletion

If you have only just deleted a file, act calmly and avoid creating new content. On a laptop, restore it from the Recycle Bin or Bin if it is present. If it is not, check your backup system first. Windows File History, macOS Time Machine and cloud backup folders can often restore an earlier version without specialist recovery work.

For an external hard drive, USB stick or memory card, unplug it once you realise important files are missing. Do not save anything else to it and do not accept a prompt to format it. You can reconnect it to inspect it, but do not write new data to it.

Recovery software can help where a device is healthy and the data has not been overwritten. It is not a guaranteed fix, especially on modern phones and solid-state drives. Many newer devices use encryption and storage management that can make permanently deleted data impossible to retrieve, even for professionals. That is why checking backups first is usually the quickest and safest option.

When a laptop or phone will not open files

A device that switches on but cannot open folders may have a software fault, a failing drive or damaged file system. Warning signs include repeated crashing, files with strange names, a drive that keeps disconnecting, clicking from a hard drive, or messages asking you to repair or format storage.

Do not format the device because it says doing so will make it usable again. Formatting can reduce the chance of recovering files. Equally, avoid running repeated repair tools if the drive is making unusual noises or regularly vanishing. A failing hard drive can deteriorate quickly with each attempt to read it.

For a phone that will not start after a drop, liquid damage or a failed update, avoid factory resetting it if the data matters. A reset may solve some software problems, but it removes local data and can make recovery much harder. Keep the device powered off if it has been exposed to water, and do not charge it until it has been assessed.

How to recover lost files from a damaged device

Physical faults need a more cautious route than simple deletion. A cracked screen does not necessarily mean the data is lost, but you may be unable to approve access, enter a passcode or transfer files. Battery faults, charging-port damage and board-level issues can create the same problem.

The aim is usually to stabilise the device and gain safe access to the data, rather than immediately attempting a full repair. This might involve restoring power, replacing a damaged screen so the device can be operated, or diagnosing storage and software faults. The best method depends on the model, its condition and whether it is encrypted.

If the device contains irreplaceable family photos, business documents, university work or travel records, professional help is often worth seeking early. Avoid these common recovery mistakes

The pressure to get files back can lead to choices that make matters worse. Avoid these four mistakes:

  • Continuing to use the device normally after deletion, including taking new photos or downloading files.
  • Installing recovery apps or updates onto the same storage that held the missing data.
  • Formatting a drive, memory card or phone because a pop-up tells you it needs fixing.
  • Factory resetting a phone or repeatedly powering on a wet or physically damaged device.

Also be wary of software that promises guaranteed recovery. No honest service can guarantee every file, because success depends on the device, the type of storage, encryption, damage and what has happened since the files disappeared.

How to tell what type of crash you have

Not every crash needs data recovery. A laptop that shuts down because of an empty battery, faulty charger or overheating problem may still have a perfectly healthy drive. Likewise, a failed screen can make it look as if the device has died when it is actually running in the background.

Software faults often appear after an update, a new application, malware or an unexpected power cut. You may see a spinning loading screen, an error message or a device that reaches the login page but freezes shortly afterwards. In some cases, files can be copied safely once the laptop starts in a recovery environment or from an external boot tool.

Hardware faults can be less predictable. Warning signs include clicking from a traditional hard drive, folders taking ages to open, files disappearing, a drive that is detected only sometimes, or a laptop that shows no internal storage at all. Solid-state drives do not usually click, but they can fail suddenly. In these situations, a proper diagnosis matters because continued use can reduce the chance of a successful recovery.

Restoring business files after a crash: the safest order

The best recovery route depends on the fault, the device and where your files were stored. However, the order below helps protect your options.

  1. Stop risky use. Switch off a device that is overheating, clicking, repeatedly crashing or failing to detect its drive. Do not keep forcing it to start.
  2. Check backups and cloud storage. Use another mobile phone, tablet or computer to look for synced folders, emailed attachments and previous versions of key documents.
  3. Record the symptoms. Note when the crash happened, whether there was a power cut or update, and any error messages. This can speed up diagnosis.
  4. Get the device assessed before resetting it. A technician can establish whether the drive is accessible, whether files can be copied, and whether the fault is software or hardware.

This approach may feel slower than pressing “reset”, but it is normally faster than trying to recover data after it has been overwritten.

Give yourself a better chance next time

Once your files are safe, take a few minutes to reduce the risk of another scare. Turn on automatic cloud backup for photos and key documents, but remember that synchronisation is not always a full backup. If you delete a file and the deletion syncs, it can disappear everywhere.

Keep a separate backup for the things you cannot replace. For most people, that means using cloud storage alongside a regular copy on an external drive. Check occasionally that the backups are actually completing and that you can open the files. It is a small habit that saves a great deal of stress.

If a missing file is important, pause before trying fixes at random. The less you change on the device, the more options you are likely to have - and the faster you can get back to work, travel or everyday life.
