Azoospermia

Azoospermia means there is no measurable sperm in the ejaculate. It is hard news to hear, but it has known causes, clear tests and more than one possible route forward. This guide explains it in plain language.

In short

  • Azoospermia means no measurable sperm in the ejaculate. It is usually confirmed by at least two semen analyses.
  • It affects about 1% of all men and around 10–15% of men with infertility.
  • There are two main types: obstructive (sperm are made but blocked) and non-obstructive (the testes make very little or no sperm).
  • Depending on the cause, options include surgical repair, sperm retrieval used with ICSI, hormone treatment for some men, or donor sperm.

What is azoospermia?

Azoospermia is the medical term for having no measurable sperm in the ejaculate. Many men only find out when they have a semen analysis as part of fertility tests.

1%

of all men are affected by azoospermia.

That rises to around 10–15% of men with infertility.

A single result is not enough for a diagnosis. Azoospermia is usually confirmed by at least two semen analyses, with the sample spun down (centrifuged) so the laboratory can look carefully for any sperm at all.

The two main types

Doctors divide azoospermia into two groups, because the cause shapes which treatments make sense.

Obstructive

Sperm are made, but blocked

The testes produce sperm, but a blockage stops them reaching the ejaculate.

Causes include a previous vasectomy, infection or injury, or congenital absence of the vas deferens, which is linked to cystic fibrosis gene changes.

Non-obstructive

Very little or no sperm made

The testes make very little or no sperm.

Causes include genetic conditions such as Klinefelter syndrome or Y-chromosome microdeletions, hormone problems, a history of undescended testes, or previous chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

How it is diagnosed

Working out the type and cause usually involves several steps. Your doctor may suggest some or all of these:

  • Repeat semen analysisAt least two samples, centrifuged and examined.
  • Physical examinationIncluding the testes and the vas deferens.
  • Hormone blood testsFSH, LH and testosterone.
  • Genetic testsKaryotype, Y-chromosome microdeletion and CFTR.
  • Scrotal ultrasoundSometimes, to look at the testes and surrounding structures.

Questions you might take to your appointment

  • Do you think my azoospermia is obstructive or non-obstructive, and why?
  • Which hormone tests have been done, and what did they show?
  • Have I had a karyotype, Y-chromosome microdeletion and CFTR test?
  • Would a scrotal ultrasound help in my case?

Treatment options

The right option depends on the type and cause. These are the main routes you are likely to hear about.

Obstructive

Repair or retrieve

Surgical repair of the blockage, such as vasectomy reversal.

Or sperm retrieval (PESA, MESA or TESA), with the sperm used in ICSI.

Non-obstructive

Hormones or micro-TESE

In some men, hormone treatment.

Surgical sperm retrieval such as micro-TESE, with any sperm found used in ICSI.

Donor sperm is another route to parenthood, and some couples consider it alongside or after other options.

Every situation is different. Talk your options through with a urologist or andrologist, who can explain what is realistic for you.
